## 2.3 The Replication Crisis as Diagnostic Evidence

**Topics to Investigate:**
- The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 *Science* paper: what they actually found, what it means, the pushback
- The crisis by field: social psychology (Diederik Stapel, Amy Cuddy's power pose, ego depletion), nutrition science, cancer biology (Amgen/Bayer replication failures), economics
- Questionable Research Practices (QRPs): p-hacking, HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known), selective outcome reporting — how widespread, how structural
- The file drawer problem: unpublished null results and how publication bias distorts the literature
- Pre-registration as reform — its adoption rate, its limitations, resistance to it from within the research community
- Fraud at the top: Diederik Stapel (social psychology), Hwang Woo-suk (stem cells), Jan Hendrik Schön (physics) — how long they operated undetected and why
- The career incentive structure: publish-or-perish, impact factor worship, citation metrics as proxy for truth — how the incentive structure guarantees replication failure
- The political valence of the replication crisis: whose ox gets gored depends on which findings don't replicate — documenting asymmetric defensiveness by ideological coalition

**Key Questions:**
- What percentage of published findings in psychology, medicine, economics, and nutrition are now known to be unreproducible? What are the best current estimates?
- What proportion of the replication crisis is attributable to fraud vs. unconscious bias vs. structural incentives vs. statistical misuse? Can these be disentangled?
- Have the proposed reforms (pre-registration, open data, registered reports) actually worked? What are the effect sizes?
- How do journal editors respond when contacted about replication failures? Are there documented cases of resistance to publishing replications?
- Who resisted the open science reforms most forcefully, and what were their institutional affiliations?
