## 2.3 The Replication Crisis as Diagnostic Evidence

### Why this section matters to the thesis

If §2.1-2.2 documents how inquiry gets killed rhetorically (poisoned well) and §3 documents how it gets killed psychologically (cult dynamics), this section documents the middle layer: how the institution that is *supposed* to be the well itself — organized science, with its peer review and its credentialing — produces false consensus at scale, and how the people who pointed this out were met with the same discrediting moves (methodological terrorism, career threats, "this damages the field") that the CIA dispatch prescribed for political dissent. The replication crisis is diagnostic because it is a case where the mechanism of manufactured consensus is *quantified* and *admitted*, not merely alleged. Unlike political conspiracy claims, the replication crisis produced its own internal audit trail: pre-registered studies, self-report surveys of researchers admitting to the practices, and a paper trail of editors and eminent scientists explicitly resisting the audit.

### 1. The Open Science Collaboration (2015): what they found

**Citation:** Open Science Collaboration, "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science," *Science* 349, no. 6251 (28 August 2015), aac4716.

- 270 researchers replicated 100 studies published in three top psychology journals (*Psychological Science*, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*) for 2008.
- 97% of the original studies reported statistically significant effects. That is the baseline: published psychology is a literature that is almost universally "positive."
- On replication, using a strict criterion (statistical significance at p < .05 in the same direction), only **36%** replicated.
- Using a softer criterion — whether the original effect size fell inside the 95% confidence interval of the replication — **47%** replicated.
- Subjective assessment by the replicating teams themselves: **39%** judged that they had replicated the original finding.
- Mean effect size in the replications was roughly **half** the magnitude of the original published effect sizes — even in the studies that "worked," the true effect was much smaller than reported.

**The pushback:** Daniel Gilbert, Gary King, Stephen Pettigrew, and Timothy Wilson published a formal technical comment in *Science* the following year — Gilbert et al., "Comment on 'Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science,'" *Science* 351, no. 6277 (4 March 2016): 1037. Their argument: the OSC replications deviated from original protocols ("infidelity"), and low-fidelity replications failed roughly four times more often than high-fidelity ones; correcting for sampling error and replication power, they argued the data were "consistent with" reproducibility being high, not low. Nosek and the OSC team responded that Gilbert et al.'s own reanalysis contained errors and that their fidelity ratings were post hoc and not blind. This is worth noting for the book's purposes: even the paper *documenting* the crisis attracted an institutional defense move — reframe the replicators as the ones who did bad science.

### 2. Field by field

**Social psychology**

- *Diederik Stapel* (Tilburg University, Netherlands): fabricated data across an estimated 15-20 years of his career. Discovery came not from peer review or journal editors but from three junior researchers in his own lab who told the dean his numbers "felt wrong" in August 2011. Within days he was suspended; within weeks he confessed. Investigating commissions found data fraud in 55 papers and 10 PhD theses he supervised; **58 retractions** are now attributed to him. The official Tilburg/Amsterdam/Groningen joint investigative report explicitly found a "sloppy" research culture in which peer reviewers *encouraged* authors to drop inconvenient data — i.e., the QRPs that produced the wider crisis were already normalized before Stapel escalated to outright fabrication. Stapel's own memoir, *Ontsporing* ("Derailed," translated as *Faking Science*), frames the motive as a compound of "the need to score, ambition, laziness, nihilism, want of power, status anxiety, desire for solutions, unity, pressure to publish, arrogance, emotional detachment, loneliness, disappointment, ADD, addiction to answers" — his own list, not a summary.
- *Amy Cuddy* (power posing): Carney, Cuddy & Yap 2010 claimed expansive postures causally raised testosterone, lowered cortisol, and increased risk tolerance — the finding behind Cuddy's 2012 TED talk, one of the most-viewed TED talks ever. By 2015-2016, multiple labs failed to replicate the hormonal and behavioral effects. In 2016, Dana Carney (the study's original lead author) posted a public statement on her own website stating she no longer believed the effect was real. Cuddy continued to defend a narrower "postural feedback on subjective feelings" version. Susan Dominus profiled the fallout in "When the Revolution Came for Amy Cuddy," *New York Times Magazine* (18 October 2017) — noting that tenured Ivy League psychologists were privately sympathetic to Cuddy but "afraid to publicly defend" her, a data point for the book's argument about reputational risk chilling dissent even among people who disagree with the mob.
- *Ego depletion* (Baumeister): the "willpower as limited resource" theory, built on decades of small studies (meta-analytic d ≈ 0.62 in the published literature), was tested in a 24-lab Registered Replication Report (Hagger et al., 2016) using a single pre-agreed protocol across >2,000 participants. Pooled effect: **d = 0.04**, CI [-0.07, 0.15] — statistically indistinguishable from zero. A second RRR (Vohs et al., 2021) used a protocol Baumeister himself pre-approved as the strongest test available, with an even larger sample, and again found no detectable effect. Baumeister's response blamed the replication design rather than the theory.
- *Social priming* (John Bargh and others): Daniel Kahneman, in a widely circulated open letter dated 26 September 2012 to priming researchers, warned of a "train wreck looming" and told them "your field is now the poster child for doubts about the integrity of psychological research" — despite describing himself as "a general believer" in the underlying effects. He called for researchers to form a replication ring to check each other's work before outsiders did it for them.

**Nutrition science**

- John Ioannidis has specifically targeted nutritional epidemiology as, in his words, a field where "the emerging picture... is difficult to reconcile with good scientific principles. The field needs radical reform" — citing self-reported dietary recall as a weak measurement instrument, small effect sizes, and a structure where "expert-driven guidelines shaped by advocates dictate what primary studies should report" rather than data shaping the guidelines.
- Pushback: Harvard's Walter Willett has argued Ioannidis "grossly exaggerat[es]" the problem and that proper statistical correction for measurement error preserves the field's informativeness. This is a live, unresolved methodological dispute, not a settled one — worth flagging as such rather than adjudicating.

**Cancer biology**

- C. Glenn Begley & Lee Ellis, "Raise Standards for Preclinical Cancer Research," *Nature* 483 (2012): 531-533. Begley, formerly VP of Hematology/Oncology Research at Amgen, reported that Amgen scientists could reproduce the original findings in only **6 of 53 landmark studies (11%)** — i.e., ~89% failed to replicate, even with author cooperation, shared reagents, and lab visits.
- Florian Prinz, Thomas Schlange & Khusru Asadullah (Bayer), "Believe It or Not: How Much Can We Rely on Published Data on Potential Drug Targets?" *Nature Reviews Drug Discovery* 10 (2011): 712. Bayer reviewed 67 in-house projects built on published target-validation findings; original data were fully reproducible in only **14 of 67 cases (~21%)**, partially in 10 more, and inconsistent/irreproducible in 43 — even after protocol adjustments.
- The formal decade-long **Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology** (Center for Open Science), final results published in *eLife* in December 2021 (Errington et al., "Investigating the Replicability of Preclinical Cancer Biology"): attempted 50 experiments from 23 high-impact 2010-2012 papers, covering 158 effects. **59% of experiments could not be replicated** by standard criteria; where original findings were positive effects, only ~40% replicated by the strictest criteria (vs. 80% for null effects). Where replication succeeded, effect sizes were on average **85% smaller** than originally published — a strikingly consistent pattern with the OSC psychology result (effect-size decay, not just significance flips).

**Economics**

- Colin Camerer et al., "Evaluating Replicability of Laboratory Experiments in Economics," *Science* 351, no. 6280 (2016): 1433-1436. Replicated 18 studies from the *American Economic Review* and *Quarterly Journal of Economics* (2011-2014), each replication pre-registered and powered at ≥90%. A significant effect in the same direction was found in **11 of 18 (61%)**; average replicated effect size was **66%** of the original. Economics thus comes out *better* than psychology or preclinical cancer biology on this metric, though still well short of full replication.

### 3. Questionable Research Practices (QRPs): how structural, how widespread

- Leslie K. John, George Loewenstein & Drazen Prelec, "Measuring the Prevalence of Questionable Research Practices with Incentives for Truth Telling," *Psychological Science* 23, no. 5 (2012): 524-532. Anonymous survey (with financial incentives for honest self-report) of over 2,000 academic psychologists. Self-admitted engagement ranged from **63%** for the mildest QRP (not reporting all dependent measures) down to **27%** for the most serious listed practice (falsely reporting that an unexpected finding had been predicted, i.e., HARKing). The authors' framing: these numbers indicate QRPs are not rare deviance but "the prevailing research norm."
- Joseph P. Simmons, Leif D. Nelson & Uri Simonsohn, "False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant," *Psychological Science* 22, no. 11 (2011): 1359-1366. Formally coined "researcher degrees of freedom." Demonstrated mathematically and empirically (including a mock study "proving" that listening to a song about age changed subjects' actual age) that ordinary, individually-defensible analytic choices — which DV to report, when to stop collecting data, which covariates to include, which conditions to combine — can inflate a nominal 5% false-positive rate to **61%** or higher without any single choice looking like fraud.
- Andrew Gelman & Eric Loken, "The Garden of Forking Paths" (Columbia working paper, later *American Scientist* 2014). Extended the Simmons/Nelson/Simonsohn critique to show the problem exists even *without* deliberate p-hacking and even when a hypothesis genuinely was set in advance: because the same hypothesis could have been tested many different defensible ways, choosing one *after seeing the data feels natural* generates the statistical equivalent of multiple comparisons, invisibly. This is the more damning version of the argument for the book's purposes — it means QRPs don't require bad faith, only normal human pattern-matching under ambiguity.
- Norbert Kerr, "HARKing: Hypothesizing After the Results Are Known," *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 2, no. 3 (1998): 196-217. Named and typologized the practice years before the crisis broke; largely ignored until 2011-2012, then became a central diagnostic term.
- Dorothy Bishop (Oxford) frames the whole cluster as "the four horsemen of irreproducibility": publication bias, low statistical power, p-hacking, and HARKing. ("Rein in the Four Horsemen of Irreproducibility," *Nature* 568 (2019): 435.)

### 4. The file-drawer problem and publication bias

- Robert Rosenthal, "The File Drawer Problem and Tolerance for Null Results," *Psychological Bulletin* 86, no. 3 (1979): 638-641. Coined the term: "journals are filled with the 5% of the studies that show Type I errors, while the file drawers are filled with the 95% of the studies that show nonsignificant results." Also introduced "fail-safe N" as a partial statistical corrective.
- Structural evidence of editorial anti-replication bias: a survey of 429 editors/editorial advisors across 19 management and social-science journals found replication studies were rejected at rates of **27% to 69%** specifically *because* they were replications; a separate audit of the 100 highest-5-year-impact-factor journals found only **~1%** of published studies were replications at all.
- Counter-evidence, for balance: a University of Michigan study found no measurable bias against novelty per se in reviewer/editor decisions — suggesting the mechanism may be more about replications being *perceived* as low-novelty/low-reward rather than a deliberate editorial vendetta against them. Worth flagging as an unresolved empirical question rather than settling it.
- Direct case study of editorial resistance: Kevin Arceneaux, Bert Bakker, Claire Gothreau & Gijs Schumacher conducted a well-powered, pre-registered replication (n=202) of a famous 2008 *Science* finding that conservatives show stronger physiological (skin-conductance) reactions to threatening images than liberals. They found **no correlation** between physiological threat-reactivity and political ideology. *Science* rejected the replication without full peer review, telling the authors "the field has moved on" and that it would be "better suited for a less visible subfield journal" — even though the editors reportedly agreed it was a conclusive replication. The authors' public complaint (Slate, June 2019): "We believe that it is bad policy for journals like *Science* to publish big, bold ideas and then leave it to subfield journals to publish replications" — because the correction never reaches the audience the original claim reached. This is a clean, well-documented instance of a flagship journal protecting a headline finding from a disconfirming replication regardless of the political valence involved (the original finding, notably, was more flattering to a liberal-coded narrative about conservative psychology) — useful case study for the book's "asymmetric defensiveness" question, though it's a case about protecting institutional prestige/narrative more than a clean left/right story.

### 5. Fraud at the top: how long, how discovered

| Case | Field | Years active before discovery | How discovered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diederik Stapel | Social psychology | ~15-20 yrs | Junior colleagues reported suspicions to department; not caught by peer review or journals |
| Jan Hendrik Schön | Condensed-matter physics, Bell Labs | ~1998-2002 (~4 yrs), 16+ retracted papers in *Science*/*Nature* | Two outside physicists noticed identical noise plots recurring across ostensibly separate experiments; Bell Labs convened an independent committee (May 2002), found falsification/fabrication in 16 of 24 reviewed cases |
| Hwang Woo-suk | Stem cell biology, Seoul National University | Claims made 2004-2005 | Korean investigative journalism (MBC) in Nov. 2005 raised egg-donation ethics questions; a collaborator (Gerald Schatten) publicly broke ties; internal SNU investigation (Jan. 2006) found no cloned stem cell lines had ever existed |

Common structural thread across all three: detection came from **insiders and informal networks noticing anomalies** (duplicated data plots, "wrong-feeling" numbers, whistleblowing junior staff), not from the formal peer-review or replication apparatus that is supposed to be science's self-correcting mechanism. Peer review, in each case, had already approved and published the fraudulent work — in top journals, for years.

**Retraction Watch database** (retractionwatch.com, maintained since 2010 by Ivan Oransky and Adam Marcus): now logs 60,000+ retractions. Annual retraction volume grew at a compound rate of roughly 22% since 2000 — more than triple the growth rate of published research overall. 2023 was a record year with over 10,000 retractions in a single year (driven heavily by paper-mill detection in bulk). Ferric Fang, R. Grant Steen & Arturo Casadevall, "Misconduct Accounts for the Majority of Retracted Scientific Publications," *PNAS* 109, no. 42 (2012): 17028-17033 — hand-reviewed all 2,047 biomedical retractions indexed to that date and found only **21.3%** attributable to honest error; **67.4%** attributable to misconduct (fraud/suspected fraud 43.4%, duplicate publication 14.2%, plagiarism 9.8%). They also found fraud-attributed retractions had risen roughly **tenfold since 1975**, and argued that earlier literature undercounted misconduct because retraction notices themselves are frequently vague or misleading about the true cause — i.e., even the correction mechanism obscures the scale of the underlying problem.

### 6. The incentive structure: does it "guarantee" replication failure?

- Chalmers & Glasziou, "Avoidable Waste in the Production and Reporting of Research Evidence," *The Lancet* 374 (2009): 86-89. Estimated cumulative ~50% loss at each of several stages (poor research questions, poor design, non-publication, poor reporting), compounding to roughly **85% avoidable waste** in biomedical research overall, an estimated $170 billion/year globally. This is a structural, not fraud-based, estimate — it indicts the system's design, not individual bad actors.
- Paul Smaldino & Richard McElreath, "The Natural Selection of Bad Science," *Royal Society Open Science* 3 (2016): 160384. Agent-based model showing that if career advancement depends on publication volume/novelty rather than methodological rigor, labs using poor (higher-false-positive) methods will out-reproduce (in the career sense) rigorous labs *even with zero deliberate misconduct* — a selection dynamic, not a virtue failure. This is the strongest single source for the "career incentive structure guarantees replication failure" claim, because it models the mechanism formally rather than asserting it.
- General "publish or perish" critique: multiple sources describe a shift from "publish or perish" to "impact or perish," with citation counts and journal impact factors repeatedly shown to be weak or even *negatively* correlated with independently assessed research quality — a textbook instance of Goodhart's Law (a measure, once made a target, ceases to be a good measure).

**Disentangling fraud vs. bias vs. incentive vs. statistics** — the honest answer, per the sources above: they are not cleanly separable, but the available evidence weights toward *structural* causes over deliberate fraud in aggregate terms. Fang et al. found ~67% of *retractions* were misconduct-driven, but retractions are a tiny, selected slice of the literature (the cases severe or visible enough to be caught and formally withdrawn). The much larger base rate of irreplicability documented by OSC, Camerer, Begley/Ellis, and the Cancer Biology Reproducibility Project occurred largely *without* retraction and largely without provable fraud — it is better explained by the QRP self-report data (John/Loewenstein/Prelec), the Gelman/Loken "garden of forking paths" mechanism (which requires no bad faith at all), and the Smaldino/McElreath incentive-selection model. In other words: fraud explains the retracted tail; QRPs plus incentive structure explain the much larger unretracted mass of irreplicable-but-still-"clean" literature. This is an inference from combining these sources' distinct findings, not a claim any single cited paper makes explicitly — flagging that synthesis as mine.

### 7. Reform: pre-registration and Registered Reports — adoption and effect

- Registered Reports (where peer review and in-principle publication acceptance happen *before* results are known) show a dramatic effect where measured: Anne Scheel, Mitchell Schijen & Daniël Lakens, "An Excess of Positive Results: Comparing the Standard Psychology Literature With Registered Reports," *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science* 4, no. 2 (2021), compared 71 Registered Reports against 152 standard papers and found **96% positive results in the standard literature vs. 44% in Registered Reports** — a 52-point gap attributed to decoupling publication decisions from outcomes.
- Adoption remains low despite the demonstrated effect: only about **7%** of psychology journals had adopted the Registered Report format as of the most recent scientometric survey cited (Springer *Scientometrics*, 2023) — the highest adoption rate of any major field, meaning the reform with the best-documented effect size remains a minority practice even in its most receptive discipline.
- Preregistration (a lighter-weight practice than full Registered Reports) grew fast in raw numbers: from 38 psychology preregistrations logged in 2012 to over 12,000 by 2017 on OSF, but this is off a near-zero base and still represents a minority of published studies.
- Brian Nosek co-founded the Center for Open Science with Jeffrey Spies in January 2013, explicitly in response to the credibility crisis exemplified by Stapel; COS built the Open Science Framework and ran both the psychology and cancer-biology Reproducibility Projects.

### 8. Institutional resistance to reform — the primary document

Susan Fiske (Princeton, former APS president), in a **leaked draft column** intended for the APS Observer (2016), branded anonymous online critics of published psychology findings as "self-appointed data police" running "denial-of-service attacks," called their conduct "methodological terrorism," and wrote that these critics "destroy lives" because they "attack the person, not just the work," warning that "colleagues at all career stages are leaving the field because of the sheer adversarial viciousness." She named no specific individuals and provided no citable instances — only "uncheckable half-anecdotes," per contemporaneous critical coverage (Neuroskeptic/Discover, and Andrew Gelman's blog, both September 2016). After public backlash the published, revised version ("A Call to Change Science's Culture of Shaming," *APS Observer*, 2016) softened the "terrorism" framing but kept the core complaint about tone. This is close to a textbook instance of the book's thesis operating inside institutional science itself: the response to a methodological critique was to reframe the critics as violent extremists rather than to engage the statistical substance — the same discrediting move (label the questioner rather than answer the question) that CIA dispatch 1035-960 prescribes for political dissent, occurring in a peer-reviewed professional association's own house organ.

### 9. Political valence — what the evidence actually shows

This is the area where the book's instructions on "don't extrapolate from sparse evidence" apply most directly, so the finding here is deliberately hedged.

- Diego Reinero, Julian Wills, William Brady, Peter Mende-Siedlecki, Jarret Crawford & Jay Van Bavel, "Is the Political Slant of Psychology Research Related to Scientific Replicability?" *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 15, no. 6 (2020): 1310-1328. Found that research with **strong political slant in either direction** (not just liberal) was less replicable than politically neutral research, and that statistical robustness (not ideology per se) predicted replication success. Because psychology as a field skews liberal in its researcher population and topic choice, and liberal-slanted work is numerically overrepresented, the *raw appearance* is that "liberal" psychology replicates worse — but the authors argue this is a sampling artifact of an underlying *symmetric* bias (politically charged findings of either stripe replicate worse), not evidence that liberal-coded findings are intrinsically less sound.
- The Arceneaux et al. replication case (§4 above) is a concrete instance where a flagship journal protected a well-known finding regardless of a rigorous disconfirming replication — but the finding being protected doesn't map cleanly onto a simple ideological story (it was a claim *about* conservatives' psychology, popular in liberal-leaning academic and media spaces, being shielded from correction).
- **What I could not find:** a rigorous, field-wide, source-backed accounting of "who resisted open-science reform most forcefully, broken out by institutional affiliation and ideological coalition." Susan Fiske's resistance and Roy Baumeister's resistance to the ego-depletion replications are the two best-documented individual cases of resistance from established researchers, but neither source frames their resistance in explicitly political/ideological terms — it reads in the primary sources as *career and reputational* defensiveness, not political defensiveness. Asserting a clean ideological-coalition pattern of resistance beyond the Reinero et al. symmetric-bias finding would be extrapolating past what these sources support. This is a gap worth flagging honestly in the manuscript rather than papering over with the first plausible-sounding narrative.
